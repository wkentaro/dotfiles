# Linguistics rubric for scoring candidate names

Score only against this document. Where evidence strength is labeled,
weigh confirmed findings over practitioner heuristics when they conflict.

## 1. Sound symbolism — empirically supported

| Sound class | Evoked qualities | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Front vowels (/i/, /e/ — "Frish") | small, light, mild, thin, soft, fast, cold, feminine | Klink 2000, *Marketing Letters* |
| Back vowels (/a/, /o/, /u/ — "Frosh") | large, heavy, strong, thick, slow, warm, masculine; smooth/creamy/rich | Klink 2000; Yorkston & Menon 2004, *JCR* |
| Fricatives (/s f v z/) | smaller, faster, lighter (vs plosives) | Klink 2000 (secondary finding) |
| Plosives (/p b t d k g/) | larger, slower, heavier; strongly overrepresented in top brands and better recalled, especially word-initial | Vanden Bergh et al. 1984, 1987; Pogacar et al. 2015 |
| Voiceless (/p t k s f/) | smaller, lighter, sharper, brighter | Klink 2000 (secondary) |
| Voiced (/b d g z v/) | larger, heavier, darker | Klink 2000 (secondary) |
| Rounded vs spiky ("bouba/kiki") | rounded sounds → soft/curvy; sharp stops + /i/ → angular/spiky | Köhler 1929; Ramachandran & Hubbard 2001 |

**Category fit beats universal preference** (Lowrey & Shrum 2007, *JCR*):
front-vowel names win where small/fast/sharp is desirable (a knife, a
convertible, a fast CLI); back-vowel names win where large/smooth/solid is
desirable (an SUV, a platform, a database). Score a name's vowels against
the framework's target qualities, not against an absolute scale.

**Processing fluency** (Alter & Oppenheimer 2006, *PNAS*): easier-to-
pronounce names are liked more and even outperformed in short-term stock
returns. Fluency is a separate lever from sound symbolism: it rewards
common phoneme sequences and easy articulation. A name can be noisy AND
fluent (Azure); complexity that fights articulation is a liability — lazy
brains lean toward what processes easily.

## 2. Practitioner letter readings — Lexicon/Placek (heuristic, not experimental)

From David Placek (Lexicon Branding; interview, Lenny's Podcast 2025).
Internal research claimed but unpublished; treat as craft priors.

- **V** — the most alive, vibrant sound in the alphabet (Vercel, Corvette, Viagra).
- **B** — among the most reliable sounds (BlackBerry's two B's).
- **Z** — noisy; a strong signal amid marketplace noise (Azure).
- **X** — fast and crisp; semantically tied to innovation.
- **S** — noisy, like Z and V (Sonos).
- Balance matters: a strong noisy signal wrapped in a smooth flow
  (A-zure: noisy Z, smooth open ending).
- Semantics ride along with sound: letters carry cultural meaning (X =
  experimental) on top of their acoustics.

**Compounds** (PowerBook, Facebook, Windsurf) are association multipliers:
two known images compound to more than their sum (1 + 1 = 3). Evidence is
practitioner-grade, not experimental — score compounds on the images they
actually summon, not on being compounds.

**Structure prior**: top brand names skew short, plosive-initial, and
alliteration-friendly (Vanden Bergh, Adler & Oliver 1987).

## 3. SMILE / SCRATCH rubric (Alexandra Watkins, *Hello, My Name Is Awesome*)

SMILE — qualities to score 0–2 each:

- **Suggestive** — hints at a benefit or attribute; evocative, not literal description.
- **Memorable** — sticks after one or two exposures: brevity, rhythm, surprise.
- **Imagery** — easy to picture; could become a logo or mental image.
- **Legs** — wordplay mileage for taglines, campaigns, culture.
- **Emotional** — creates a feeling or personality, not just a label.

SCRATCH — veto flags; any one sinks a name. Apply only when genuinely true:

- **Spelling-challenged** — can't be spelled from hearing it aloud.
- **Copycat** — too close to a competitor; no distinctiveness, weak trademark.
- **Restrictive** — boxes into one product; no room to grow.
- **Annoying/Awkward** — forced, contrived, awkward on the tongue.
- **Tame** — flat, timid, descriptive; no spark (includes faddish -ly/-ify suffixes that date fast).
- **Curse of knowledge / initials** — only makes sense knowing the product already, or its initials spell something bad.

A descriptive name is Tame by definition: it imitates the category's
language and forfeits the distinctiveness that compounds into cumulative
advantage. Comfortable names score low on purpose.

## 4. Japanese section — katakana readiness and reverse risk

Japanese is a strict CV-mora language: consonant clusters get epenthetic
vowels (usually *u*; *o* after t/d), every final consonant except N gains
a vowel. "Script" → スクリプト (5 morae). Poor mappings: l/r merge
(raito = light *and* right); v → b in practice (ヴ is written but spoken
as b); th → s/z; si → shi, ti → chi, tu → tsu; f only as fu.

**Score well**: names resolving to ≤5 morae in katakana, ideally
vowel-final (Sony ソニー, Sega セガ, Uber ウーバー) — they read as already
Japanese. Natural long vowels (ー) or gemination (っ) read deliberately
branded. Crisp k/s/t = clean, premium, tech-forward; soft m/n/y + long
vowels = warm, consumer-friendly.

**Flag**: names whose clusters balloon the mora count (Squarespace →
スクエアスペース, 8 morae); names with multiple plausible katakana
renderings (ヴ vs b, optional long vowels — splits search and recall;
cf. Tumblr vs タンブラー the cup).

**Kill-scan the katakana aloud** against: baka, kuso, chin-/-chin, man-
(initial), unko/unchi, hentai, oppai, shine (死ね vs English "shine"),
kimo-, dasai, debu/busu/aho. Siri = 尻 (shiri, butt) is the canonical miss.

**Romaji reverse-check** — read the Japanese pronunciation as an English
speaker would: Calpis → "cow piss" (renamed Calpico abroad), Creap →
"creep", Kinki → "kinky". Three-way read: English pronunciation,
katakana-Japanese pronunciation, romaji-as-English; a landmine in any of
the three is at least a flag.

Sources: Japanese phonology (Wikipedia), Tofugu loanword phonology,
Calpis/Kit Kats in Japan (Wikipedia), SoraNews24 & Kotaku round-ups of
cross-language brand failures.

## 5. Scoring procedure

For each name: (1) SMILE subscores 0–2 with the definitions above;
(2) SCRATCH flags only where genuinely true; (3) sound analysis — map its
vowels/consonants through §1–2 against the framework's target qualities,
rewarding category fit per Lowrey & Shrum; (4) fluency 0–2 per §1;
(5) total = SMILE sum + fluency (0–12). Japanese checks (§4) belong to
the cultural screen, not the score.
